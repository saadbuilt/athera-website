§ 01

The cooling crisis is accelerating.

Pakistan lives with extreme summer heat, rising cooling demand and an electricity grid that is already under strain. Every day, buildings soak up solar heat through their roofs, walls and windows — and then we spend electricity removing it again.

The result is a destructive loop. Air conditioners dump their heat back into the street; every kilowatt they draw adds emissions. Dense urban areas can run 3–5 °C hotter than the surrounding countryside, driven by heat-absorbing surfaces and the waste heat of the machines meant to fix them.

Cooling our buildings heats our cities — and fuels the very crisis we are trying to solve.

The most sustainable cooling strategy is not a bigger air conditioner. It is stopping buildings from absorbing heat in the first place.

Environmental

Rising urban temperatures, heat-island effects, growing emissions and deep dependence on mechanical cooling.

Economic

High electricity bills, climbing cooling spend, grid pressure and long-term operating costs for commercial buildings.

Social

Poor thermal comfort, heat exposure in schools and hospitals, and greater vulnerability where power is unreliable.

§ 02

Three layers. One continuous shield.

ATHERA is not a roof tile or a paint. Others address individual sources of heat; ATHERA treats the building envelope as one integrated cooling system. Each layer uses a different property of titanium dioxide (TiO2) to reflect, scatter or radiate heat — and the roof doubles as a rainwater catchment.

A

TiO2 ventilated roof tiles

Anatase TiO2-coated ceramic tiles reflect part of the incoming solar radiation. Beneath them, a ventilated air gap lets warmed air rise and escape, carrying heat away from the ceiling slab by natural convection — no fans, no electricity.

  • Solar reflectance
  • Passive convective ventilation gap
  • Photocatalytic self-cleaning to slow albedo decay
B

TiO2 exterior wall coating

A rutile TiO2 coating that reflects and backscatters UV and near-infrared rays before the wall absorbs them — designed to block over 50% of solar heat gain at the wall surface — while photocatalytic oxidation breaks down certain NOx and VOC pollutants.

  • Rutile TiO2 for high refractive index
  • Photocatalytic air-purifying action
  • Reflective, low-maintenance finish
C

Flexible radiative window films

TiO2 nanoparticles in a PDMS (polydimethylsiloxane) matrix scatter incoming solar heat while letting daylight through. The PDMS host is designed to radiate heat outward as long-wave infrared through the atmospheric transparency window.

  • High visible-light transmission
  • Passive radiative heat rejection
  • Flexible, retrofit-friendly application
D

Integrated rainwater harvesting

The roof becomes a catchment. A 30,000-litre storage system captures rainwater for non-potable building uses — a second sustainability benefit, beyond cooling, from the same roof works.

  • 30,000 L storage capacity
  • Non-potable uses: flushing, cleaning, irrigation
  • Built into the roof & water-capture package

Why self-cleaning matters

Reflective roofs usually fail slowly. Dust, soot and grime darken them, and every lost point of albedo is heat let back in.

Under suitable light, anatase TiO2 generates reactive species that break down organic dirt on its surface. The aim is a roof that stays reflective for longer with less maintenance — a property we will confirm through outdoor exposure testing.

§ 03

Reflect the sun.
Radiate to space.

Sunlight arrives mostly between 0.3 and 2.5 µm. A warm surface glows back in the far infrared — and between roughly 8 and 13 µm the atmosphere is nearly transparent, so heat emitted in that band can escape toward the cold of space.

0.3–0.4 µm

Ultraviolet

Backscattered by the rutile wall coating — and used by anatase surfaces to drive self-cleaning photocatalysis.

0.4–0.7 µm

Visible

Reflected off roofs and walls; transmitted through the window film so interiors keep their daylight and views.

0.7–2.5 µm

Near-infrared

Roughly half of the sun’s energy. Scattered by TiO2 particles before it can warm the envelope.

8–13 µm

Long-wave IR

The band the PDMS film is engineered to emit in — heat that leaves through the sky’s transparency window.

Engineering note Radiative cooling is not automatic. A film’s solar reflectance, visible transmission and infrared emissivity have to be engineered together — and measured. That testing is part of our plan (§ 07).

Questions

Asked often, answered plainly.

What is TiO2?

Titanium dioxide is a widely used white inorganic compound with strong light-scattering properties, found in paints, coatings and many industrial products. Certain crystal forms are also photocatalytic under suitable light. It is well characterised and non-toxic in these applications.

What’s the difference between anatase and rutile?

They are two crystal structures of titanium dioxide. Anatase is known for strong photocatalytic activity, so we use it on the roof tiles for self-cleaning. Rutile has a higher refractive index and excellent optical scattering, so we use it in the wall coating for reflectance.

What is photocatalytic self-cleaning?

When TiO2 absorbs suitable light it generates reactive species that break down certain organic contaminants on its surface. That slows the build-up of organic dirt that would otherwise darken a reflective surface.

What are NOx and VOCs?

Nitrogen oxides (NOx) are pollutants produced mainly by high-temperature combustion such as vehicle engines and industry. Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are carbon-based chemicals that evaporate easily, from sources like solvents and paints.

How does the roof ventilation gap work?

Heat warms the air under the tiles. Warm air rises and leaves through the ventilated space while cooler air is drawn in behind it. It’s natural convection — no fans, no electricity.

What is radiative cooling?

Every warm surface sheds heat as infrared radiation. Between about 8 and 13 µm the atmosphere is largely transparent, so heat emitted in that band can escape toward space. Our window film is designed to emit strongly in that band — a property we will verify with emissivity testing.

How is this different from an air conditioner?

An air conditioner uses electricity to remove heat that has already entered. ATHERA reduces the heat that gets in, so the air conditioner has less to do. It complements cooling equipment rather than replacing it outright.

Can it go on existing buildings?

Yes. ATHERA is modular and retrofit-oriented. Components can be installed individually or together, depending on the building.

What if a customer can’t afford the full system?

Start with the surface where heat gain is greatest — often the roof — and add the walls and windows later. The assessment tells us where to begin.

Are the performance numbers proven?

Not yet, and we say so. They are design targets and model assumptions. Pilot buildings with metered energy use and independent lab testing are how we will prove them — see § 07.
